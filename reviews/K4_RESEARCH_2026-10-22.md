# Erdős 727: audit of k=2/k=3 and research toward k=4

22 October 2026. Base revision:
`8c1e3e50040224b5d3394f91f8c491be8307cd47`.

**Status: k=4 is not proved here.** This continuation supplies exact quartic
constructions, a new analytic estimate for primes below the parameter height,
independently checked finite certificates, and a precise account of the missing
large-prime estimate. None of the scans, residue tables, or heuristic comparisons
is an infinitude proof. The new analytic work has not received outside human
review or proof-assistant certification.

## 2. Why the same quadratic construction cannot reach k=4

**Proposition.** There is no quadratic polynomial `F` over the rationals, with
nonzero leading coefficient, for which `F+1,F+2,F+3,F+4` all split over the
rationals.

Write `F(t)=At²+Bt+C`. If all four translates split, their discriminants

\[
B^2-4A(C+1),\ B^2-4A(C+2),\ B^2-4A(C+3),\ B^2-4A(C+4)
\]

are four rational squares in a nonconstant arithmetic progression. Clearing
square denominators contradicts Fermat's four-squares theorem. See
[van der Poorten's proof](https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.3850).

This excludes every proposed quadratic with eight rational linear factors,
independently of a denominator search. It does not exclude k=4 itself.

## 3. An explicit quartic continuation

Let

\[
x=210t+14,\qquad
n=\frac{x^4+419x^2+43260}{210},\qquad t\ge0.
\]

This is integral: it equals `m(m+1)/210−3` with `m=x²+209≡195 (mod210)`.
All four consecutive translates factor into quadratic polynomials:

\[
\begin{aligned}
210(n+1)&=(x^2+189)(x^2+230),\\
210(n+2)&=(x^2+195)(x^2+224),\\
210(n+3)&=(x^2+209)(x^2+210),\\
210(n+4)&=(x^2-x+210)(x^2+x+210).
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

These identities are exact. They solve the algebraic factorization problem but
introduce prime divisors as large as the square of the parameter. That change
is the central analytic issue.

### A certified example

At `t=8`, one has `x=1694` and

\[
n=39\,219\,110\,444,
\qquad
\bigl(39\,219\,110\,448!\bigr)^2
 \mid 78\,438\,220\,888!.
\]

For every prime dividing `(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)(n+4)`, the certificate verifies

\[
v_p\binom{2n}{n}\ge
2v_p\bigl((n+1)(n+2)(n+3)(n+4)\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

It checks this both by Legendre's formula and by direct base-p addition.
Primes outside that product require no additional check because their
factorial margin equals their nonnegative central-binomial valuation.
The witness is not claimed to be the smallest k=4 solution.

### A deterministic large-prime lemma

For `x≡14 (mod210)`, `x≥14`, define

\[
B(x)=\frac{x^3-x^2+210x-420}{7}.
\]

If a prime `p` divides `n+4` and `p²>B(x)`, then
`v_p(n+4)=1` and `v_p(binomial(2n,n))≥2`. Such a prime cannot cause a
k=4 failure.

To see the second carry, write `x²+σx+210=ap`, with `σ=±1`. The exact identity is

\[
\frac n{p^2}=\frac{a^2}{210}
 -\frac{\sigma ax}{105p}-\frac4{p^2}.
\tag{3}
\]

For the plus factor, `a²≡0 (mod210)` and the small correction is negative.
For the minus factor, `a²` is `154` or `196` modulo `210`; the correction is
positive. The stated bound keeps the resulting fractional part in `[1/2,1)`.
Also, `B(x)` exceeds both quadratic factors, ruling out a square prime divisor.
The finite exceptional common-factor primes are below this bound. The first
carry follows from `n≡−4 (modp)`.

The complete inequalities and finite verification are in
[`construction-exploration.md`](../research/k4/construction-exploration.md).

## 4. The best developed analytic family

The stronger family for the present analytic method is

\[
D=63\,987\,840=210\cdot552^2,
\qquad y\equiv23\,971\pmod D,
\]
\[
m=\frac{y^2-1681}{552},\qquad
n=F(y)=\frac{m(m+1)}{210}-3
 =\frac{y^4-2810y^2-190065671}{D}.
\tag{4}
\]

Its exact factorizations are

\[
\begin{aligned}
D(n+1)&=(y^2-12721)(y^2+9911),\\
D(n+2)&=(y-97)(y+97)(y^2+6599),\\
D(n+3)&=(y-41)(y+41)(y^2-1129),\\
D(n+4)&=(y^2-138y+8117)(y^2+138y+8117).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

There are four linear factors, four even irreducible quadratics, and two
irreducible quadratics with a nonzero linear term.

### Exact stable-carry counts for the linear factors

For each root `r∈{−97,−41,41,97}`, put `y=r+ap`. Its exact Taylor expansion is

\[
F(y)=\frac{a^4p^4+4ra^3p^3+(6r^2-2810)a^2p^2
 +r(4r^2-5620)ap}{D}-i,
\]

where `i=2` for `|r|=97` and `i=3` for `|r|=41`.
On the fixed progression, the leading fractions at carry levels 2, 3 and 4
are fixed by `p mod D`. Their endpoint corrections were included in the count.

| Root r | No carry at 2 | No carry at both 2,3 | No carry at all 2,3,4 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| −97 | 50/99 | 25/99 | 1729/13662 |
| −41 | 1/3 | 1/6 | 0 |
| 41 | 49/99 | 49/198 | 94/759 |
| 97 | 1/3 | 1/6 | 0 |
| Sum | **5/3** | **5/6** | **311/1242** |

Two independent enumerations checked all `φ(D)=13,989,888` unit classes.
These are exact joint counts, not independence estimates. The small correction
stabilizes at levels 2,3,4 when respectively
`p>Y^(1/2+η), p>Y^(2/3+η), p>Y^(3/4+η)` for `y∈[Y,2Y]`.

### New averaged carry estimate for the quadratic factors

The additional external input is quadratic-root equidistribution over primes
in a fixed arithmetic progression: Tóth, Theorem 6.1.3 in
[his dissertation](https://real-d.mtak.hu/1120/7/dc_1553_18_doktori_mu.pdf),
printed page 96. Both positive and negative nonsquare discriminants are covered.

For an even quartic `(y⁴+By²+C)/D` and an irreducible factor
`g(y)=y²+uy+v`, restrict to a root class and write `y=R+Mpb`, where `D|M`.
After removing integer-valued polynomial terms, the leading coefficients for
carry levels 2,3,4 are respectively

\[
\frac MD\frac{(4u^2-4v+2B)r+4uv}{p},\qquad
\frac{M^2}{D}\frac{-6ur+B-6v}{p},\qquad
\frac{4M^3}{D}\frac r p.
\tag{6}
\]

Here `r` is the root modulo `p`, and all constants are fixed. In this family
the coefficient of `r` in the first expression is nonzero.

Repeated finite differencing reduces each fixed Fourier frequency to finitely
many linear sums. Excluding fixed small arcs around rational leading
coefficients makes those sums tend to zero as the progression length tends
to infinity. Tóth's theorem bounds the weighted mass of the excluded arcs.
The order is height to infinity, then arc width to zero, then differencing
cutoff to infinity. Lower phase coefficients may vary arbitrarily.

Consequently levels 2 and 4 are jointly equidistributed in the prime-root
average for `p<Y^(1−η)`. Level 3 is available in that entire range when
`u≠0`. For even quadratic factors it is available only below `Y^(2/3−η)`
by the separate Weyl estimate. Higher levels use the quartic Weyl/derivative
estimate up to `p<Y^(4/J−η)`.

This proof uses roots modulo `p`, not an unproved equidistribution statement
for roots modulo `p²`. A separate challenge checked the root representatives,
joint Fourier modes, reciprocal-prime weighting, and ordered limits:
[`K4_DFI_INDEPENDENT_CHECK_2026-10-22.md`](K4_DFI_INDEPENDENT_CHECK_2026-10-22.md).

### What the estimate actually proves

Let `T₆=Σ(J≥6) 2^(1−J) log((J+1)/J)`. The failure cost from the six
quadratic factors, for primes below the parameter height, is at most

\[
C_{\rm quad}=6T_6+\frac58\log\frac65+\frac54\log\frac54
 =0.44435772291501785\ldots.
\]

For the four linear factors the exact joint table gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
C_{\rm lin}={}&4\sum_{J\ge8}2^{1-J}\log\frac{J+1}{J}
 +\frac53\sum_{J=6}^7 2^{2-J}\log\frac{J+1}{J}\\
&+\frac5{24}\log\frac98
 +\frac{311}{2484}\log\frac{16}{15}
 +\frac{311}{1242}\log\frac54\\
={}&0.1182099412734677\ldots.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the derived upper limiting failure proportion for these controlled prime
ranges is

\[
C_{\rm quad}+C_{\rm lin}=0.5625676641884855292\ldots.
\tag{7}
\]

More explicitly, among admissible `y∈[Y,2Y]`, a lower limiting proportion at
least `0.4374323358115144707…` satisfies (2) for every prime `p≤y`. Fixed primes,
repeated factors, and small growing primes have already been included through
the cutoff-and-limit arguments. Narrow exponent strips around `p≈Y` can be
bounded on the `p≤2Y` side and then removed. All linear-factor primes are also
covered, apart from negligible endpoint ranges.

**This is a partial valuation result. It does not say that that proportion
satisfies the factorial divisibility.** Primes exceeding `y` in the quadratic
factors remain.

The full proof and exact linear analysis are in
[`analytic-audit.md`](../research/k4/analytic-audit.md), Section 5, and
[`construction-linear-analysis.md`](../research/k4/construction-linear-analysis.md).

## 5. The remaining gap, stated quantitatively

To complete this particular union-bound argument, one needs an upper bound
strictly below

\[
0.4374323358115144707\ldots
\]

for the proportion of parameters with an obstructing prime `p>y` from the
quadratic factors in (5), or a different argument that handles their overlap
with the already bounded failures.

The old k=3 proof counts points in a prime root class by `Y/p+O(1)`. Its
linear factors have prime divisors `p=O(Y)`, so summing the endpoint errors
costs only `O(Y/logY)`. A quadratic factor can have a prime divisor of size
`O(Y²)`. Summing the same `O(1)` error up to that height is not negligible.
Neither a good residue table nor Mertens' theorem repairs this loss.

For (5), the stabilized large-prime bad coefficient is `1/6` from the old even
quadratics and `1/3` from the new odd pair. These are averages over prime
residue classes, not proved densities of prime divisors of polynomial values
in a short parameter interval.

Even the optimistic, **unproved** independent-distribution calculation gives
an additional failure cost about `0.4490691201`, putting the total near
`1.0116367843`. That number is not a bound. It shows that simply replacing the
missing step by the hoped-for random model would still leave a small deficit
for this particular union bound. A genuine improvement is needed in addition
to a rigorous large-prime estimate.

Published smooth-value results do not automatically solve this problem: a
positive-density smooth-value set and a positive-density carry-success set
need not intersect. Also, the 2025 Grimmelt–Merikoski quadratic sieve theorem
is stated for negative discriminants; it cannot simply be applied to the
positive-discriminant alternatives below. The detailed source assessment is
in the three `K4_*` review notes linked in Section 8.

## 6. Other constructions and searches

An additional exact family has eight linear factors and only four quadratic
factors:

\[
G(y)=\frac{y^4-810y^2+2240y-41751}{40320}.
\]
\[
\begin{aligned}
40320(G+1)&=(y-1)(y-27)(y^2+28y-53),\\
40320(G+2)&=(y-9)(y+29)(y^2-20y-149),\\
40320(G+3)&=(y-13)(y+9)(y^2+4y-677),\\
40320(G+4)&=(y-19)(y+27)(y^2-8y-233).
\end{aligned}
\]

For example, `y≡1 (mod40320)` makes `G(y)` integral, and `G(y)>0` eventually.
Every remaining quadratic has a nonzero linear term, so all three carry
coordinates 2,3,4 are available below the parameter height. However, its
optimized stable large-prime residue coefficients are worse. More linear
factors alone do not produce a proof.

We also investigated the downward extension

\[
m=(y^2-14401)/432,\qquad n=m(m+1)/210-4.
\]

It has six linear factors and five quadratic factors. Its improved stabilized
large-prime coefficient `1/3` is offset by a linear-factor failure cost about
`0.3064786812`; its full heuristic total still exceeds one.

The finite construction searches include:

* A cubic split-fiber search through `A=100,000,000`, checking 30,237,841
  representations `A=r²+rs+s²`, with no four-term AP of the required constants.
* An exact non-even quartic search through the stated `u≤250` bounds:
  1,906,593,000 orderings, 127,846 rational-square hits, and 87,016 primitive
  distinct coefficient tuples. No tuple had five or more split quadratic
  factors; one had four.
* Exact signed norm searches with numerator and denominator bounded by
  10,000, including a separate search for any three of the six old factors
  splitting, and complete residue refinements for the leading candidates.
* A targeted rational-parameter search with numerator and denominator at most
  5,000.

These are bounded exclusions, not nonexistence theorems. Search sources,
arguments, exact limits, and receipts are retained under `research/k4/`.
